## 2. What partial volume correction does to the images

Measured per frame, then taken as a median over all frames and scans. Peak
recovery is the blood-pool peak after correction divided by before; noise
amplification is the same ratio for the within-region percentage standard
deviation.

| Input tag | Peak recovery | Noise amplification | Negative voxels after |
|---|---|---|---|
| `rl_i10` | 1.554 | 1.862 | 0.000 |
| `rl_i15` | 1.598 | 2.004 | 0.000 |
| `rl_i20` | 1.620 | 2.090 | 0.000 |
| `rvc_i10` | 1.541 | 1.997 | 0.000 |
| `rvc_i15` | 1.593 | 2.109 | 0.000 |
| `rvc_i20` | 1.627 | 2.173 | 0.000 |

The correction works, and it costs what deconvolution always costs: roughly
60 % more peak signal for roughly twice the noise. More iterations buy a little
more peak and a little more noise, monotonically, with no turning point inside
the range tested. Richardson–Lucy and Van-Cittert behave almost identically at
matched iteration counts; RL is marginally quieter. No negative voxels are
produced by either.

This is the input-side result and it stands on its own. Everything in §3 is
about what the network then does with these images.

## 3. Main result: PVC helps, by about 4 %, and only on the diagonal

Median RMSE per condition, and the paired difference against the condition it
can legitimately be read against. Every comparison is over the same 70 scans.

| Condition | Median RMSE | Δ vs its reference | 95 % CI | *r* | *p* (Holm) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **`baseline_retrained`** | **0.580** | — | — | — | — |
| `rvc_retrained` | 0.524 | **−0.024** | [−0.083, −0.009] | −0.35 | **0.023** |
| `rl_retrained` | 0.526 | **−0.020** | [−0.056, +0.004] | −0.29 | **0.032** |
| `mc_baseline_shift_retrained` | 0.571 | +0.011 | [−0.004, +0.022] | 0.11 | 0.445 |
| `mc_rl_pvcfirst_retrained` | 0.714 | +0.108 | [0.065, 0.160] | 0.75 | < 0.001 |
| `mc_rl_mcfirst_retrained` | 0.737 | +0.115 | [0.064, 0.179] | 0.76 | < 0.001 |
| `rvc_shift_retrained` | 0.804 | +0.167 | [0.123, 0.242] | 0.89 | < 0.001 |
| `rl_shift_retrained` | 0.803 | +0.174 | [0.124, 0.233] | 0.87 | < 0.001 |
| `rl_reverse_shift_retrained` | 0.823 | +0.331 | [0.273, 0.391] | 0.94 | < 0.001 |
| `rvc_reverse_shift_retrained` | 0.856 | +0.370 | [0.325, 0.420] | 0.96 | < 0.001 |

Paired Wilcoxon signed-rank; Holm-corrected within each arm (see §3.4); CI by
percentile bootstrap over 10 000 resamples; effect size is rank-biserial.

**Retraining on corrected images improves the predicted input function.** RVC
by 0.024 and RL by 0.020, both significant. The effect is small — *r* around
0.3, an RMSE reduction of roughly 4 % — and RVC is the cleaner of the two
because its confidence interval excludes zero while RL's does not quite. Where
the interval and the test disagree, both are reported: they estimate different
things, and preferring the one that reads better would be a choice made on the
outcome.

At three runs per condition these same comparisons read −0.016 and +0.003,
neither significant. Completing the grid is what produced the result.

### 3.1 The full two-by-two of training against test representation

The four cells, with each off-diagonal read against the condition that shares
its weights:

| | test on `orig` | test on `rl_i15` |
|---|---|---|
| **trained on `orig`** | 0.580 | 0.803 **(+0.174)** |
| **trained on `rl_i15`** | 0.823 **(+0.331)** | 0.526 |

Two things follow.

**Mismatch is not symmetric, and it is worse in the direction nobody expects.**
Training on the corrected images — sharper edges, roughly twice the noise —
does not buy robustness to smoother ones. It costs about twice what the forward
mismatch costs. The domain-randomisation intuition, that fitting on the harder
domain generalises downward, is falsified here.

**Both off-diagonal cells land at about the same absolute RMSE**, 0.80 to 0.86.
Mismatch puts the model in the same bad place whichever way it is crossed; the
deltas differ because the reverse direction starts from a better diagonal and
therefore throws more away.

### 3.2 The two directions of mismatch fail differently

Identical total error, composed almost oppositely:

| | MSE | Bias² | Variance | Var share | Signed bias |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `baseline_retrained` | 0.569 | 0.352 | 0.217 | 38 % | −0.079 |
| `rl_shift` (clean → sharp) | 0.940 | 0.462 | **0.478** | **51 %** | **+0.197** |
| `rl_reverse_shift` (sharp → clean) | 0.939 | **0.784** | 0.155 | **16 %** | **−0.482** |

0.940 against 0.939, and the resemblance ends there. The forward mismatch is
variance; the reverse is bias, with a variance *lower* than the reference's.

The mechanism is legible. Shown sharper images than it was fitted on, the model
has detail it never learned to use: no systematic wrong answer, just
instability, independently trained models disagreeing with one another. Shown
blurrier images than it was fitted on, the features it learned to depend on are
absent: every model is wrong, and wrong in the same direction. Consistently
wrong is the more expensive failure.

### 3.3 The peak, in both directions

Median predicted peak height as a fraction of the arterial truth, and the
predicted full width at half maximum against a true width of about 0.65 min:

| Condition | Peak height ratio | Predicted FWHM |
|---|---|---|
| `baseline_retrained` | 0.949 | 0.653 |
| `rl_retrained` | 0.954 | 0.677 |
| `rl_shift_retrained` | **1.051** | 0.645 |
| `rl_reverse_shift_retrained` | **0.721** | **1.141** |

This is the evidence for the double-correction account. DLIF fitted against
arterial ground truth has already learned to undo partial volume implicitly —
mapping a PV-affected image to the true input function is precisely what it was
trained to do. A model fitted on images where that correction had *already been
applied* has learned to do less of it. Show it uncorrected images and it
under-corrects: a quarter of the peak missing and the bolus smeared to nearly
twice its true width. Show the opposite model corrected images and it corrects
twice, overshooting by 5 %.

Two opposite predictions from one mechanism, both borne out. Worth noting that
the prediction was made before the reverse conditions were run.

### 3.4 A note on the correction family

*p*-values are Holm-corrected within each arm rather than across all
comparisons at once. The arms answer different questions against different
references, and correcting across them made the main hypothesis' *p*-value
depend on how many sensitivity conditions happened to be run on the deployed
model — a quantity no reader of that hypothesis would take into account.
Under the previous all-in-one-family scheme `rl_retrained` moved from 0.26 to
0.36 purely because PSF conditions were added elsewhere in the study.

RMSE is the primary endpoint; the remaining metrics are secondary and the
sensitivity grids are descriptive. The ratio metrics (`auc_ratio`,
`peak_height_ratio`) are right at 1.0 rather than at zero, so "lower" is not
"better" for them and the figures that display them mark the target explicitly.

## 4. Where the improvement comes from

### 4.1 It is mostly reduced variance

| Condition | Repeats | MSE | Bias² | Variance | Var share |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `baseline_retrained` | 10 | 0.569 | 0.352 | **0.217** | 38 % |
| `rl_retrained` | 10 | 0.433 | 0.308 | **0.125** | 29 % |
| `rvc_retrained` | 10 | 0.431 | 0.322 | **0.109** | 25 % |

Variance roughly halves. Of the MSE reduction, 68 % (RL) and 78 % (RVC) is
variance rather than bias.

This survived the obvious objection. At three runs `rl_retrained` showed a
variance of 0.098; with ten it rose to 0.125, as an under-sampled variance
estimate should, and still sat far below the reference's 0.217. The effect was
not an artefact of the repeat count.

Set beside §3.2 the study now has a symmetric pair along one axis:

| | Δ MSE | Variance share of the change |
|---|---|---|
| Fitted on uncorrected → shown corrected | **+0.371** | 70 % |
| Fitted on corrected → shown corrected | **−0.136** | 68 % |

What governs the network's behaviour is whether the model and the input
representation match. Mismatch in the forward direction makes the predictions
scatter; matching makes them agree — more than it makes them accurate.

Two consistency checks support that reading rather than a bias reading. The
predicted peak height barely moves (0.954 against 0.949) and neither does the
predicted width, so the gain is not better peak recovery. And CCC and R² are
flat or a hair lower (0.962 / 0.923 against 0.964 / 0.927): the average
prediction is barely better, while the spread between runs is much smaller.
That is exactly the signature of a variance-dominated improvement.

### 4.2 Where along the curve it lands

RMSE by time bin:

| Bin | `baseline_retrained` | `rl_retrained` | `rvc_retrained` |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0–1 min | 0.895 | 0.867 | 0.867 |
| **1–2.5 min** | **0.961** | **0.801** | **0.799** |
| 2.5–5.5 min | 0.342 | 0.321 | 0.315 |
| 5.5–40.5 min | 0.271 | 0.229 | 0.232 |
| ≥ 40.5 min | 0.323 | 0.253 | 0.242 |

Two readings of this table differ, and only one of them survives.

In **absolute** terms the bolus window dominates: 0.159 of the total
improvement is in 1–2.5 minutes, against 0.070 in the tail and 0.021–0.042
elsewhere. Since the aggregate RMSE is an absolute quantity, that bin is where
most of the headline effect comes from.

In **relative** terms the improvement is broad, and the peak is not where it is
largest:

| Bin | Absolute gain | Relative gain |
|---|---|---|
| 0–1 min | 0.028 | 3.1 % |
| 1–2.5 min | **0.159** | 16.6 % |
| 2.5–5.5 min | 0.021 | 6.1 % |
| 5.5–40.5 min | 0.042 | 15.7 % |
| ≥ 40.5 min | 0.070 | **21.7 %** |

The bolus bin carries the most absolute error to begin with — 0.961 against
0.271 in the late frames — so an improvement landing there is partly a
consequence of that being where the error lives. The tail improves by more
proportionally.

So this is **not** a peak-specific effect, and it should not be described as
one. It is a broad improvement that shows up largest in absolute terms where
the errors are largest.

What does improve specifically at the peak is reliability rather than
amplitude. The predicted peak height is essentially unchanged — 7.36 SUV
against the reference's 7.51, on a true peak of 8.01 — so the prediction is not
taller, it is more consistently right.

**On the loss weighting.** The published `WeightedMSELoss` splits the curve at
frames 0/25/34/42 with weights 0.4/0.7/1.0, each segment averaged over its own
frames before weighting; per frame that is 0.016 / 0.078 / 0.125, weighting the
late equilibrium frames about eight times as heavily as the bolus. This is
worth recording as a property of the protocol. It is *not* supported by the
table above as a diagnosis: the relative improvement in the heavily-weighted
late frames is the largest of any bin, which is the opposite of what a
"the loss ignores the region that matters" account would predict. Whether a
different weighting helps is an open question this study does not answer.

### 4.3 Downstream: group bias improves, per-animal accuracy does not

Patlak *K*ᵢ, relative error against the same model driven by arterial truth:

| | Brain | Liver | Myocardium |
|---|---|---|---|
| `baseline_retrained`, median | −5.3 % | −29.2 % | −2.7 % |
| `rl_retrained` | −4.0 % | −18.4 % | −0.9 % |
| `rvc_retrained` | −2.2 % | −17.4 % | +0.8 % |
| `baseline_retrained`, median \|error\| | 12.6 % | 37.4 % | 9.4 % |
| `rl_retrained` | 13.9 % | 35.6 % | 11.0 % |
| `rvc_retrained` | 13.3 % | 39.5 % | 10.7 % |

Group-level bias clearly improves: RVC more than halves the brain bias and
takes the myocardium through zero. Per-animal absolute error does not, and the
liver interquartile range widens from 51 to 57 points.

So PVC removes a systematic underestimation of *K*ᵢ across the cohort without
making any individual animal's *K*ᵢ more accurate. That is a real result and a
considerably more modest one than "PVC improves kinetic modelling".

One warning sits in the same table. `rl_shift_retrained` has the best liver
*K*ᵢ bias of any condition (−12.4 %) and by a wide margin the worst input
function. Selecting a method on a downstream summary statistic would have
chosen the worst curve in the study.

## 5. Motion

### 5.1 How much there is

FALCON, rigid registration, reference frame 34, registration started at frame 5.
69 scans, 2484 registered frames.

| Quantity | Median | p95 | Max |
|---|---|---|---|
| Per-frame translation | 0.271 mm | 0.852 mm | 4.714 mm |
| Per-frame rotation | 0.334° | 1.231° | 6.645° |
| Per-scan median translation | 0.280 mm | — | 0.491 mm |
| Per-scan max translation | 0.995 mm | — | 4.714 mm |

Voxels are 0.5 × 0.5 × 0.597 mm, so the median frame moves about half a voxel.
All 69 scans exceed 0.5 mm at some frame and 33 of 69 exceed 1 mm. The motion
is real, it is present everywhere, and it is small.

Only rigid registration is used, deliberately. Affine and deformable are not
volume-preserving, and the quantity of interest is a concentration — a
transform that changes a region's volume changes its SUV directly.

### 5.2 What correcting it costs

| Quantity | Before | After | Ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gradient energy | 0.1057 | 0.0863 | **0.814** |
| High-frequency share | 0.538 | 0.483 | 0.898 |

Motion correction removes **18.6 % of the image's gradient energy**. That is
interpolation smoothing, and it is the cost side of the ledger: the corrected
series is measurably blurrier than the original. Set against a median
displacement of half a voxel, it is not obvious that the trade is worth making.

### 5.3 It is not worth making

`mc_baseline_shift_retrained` isolates the registration: the same weights as
the reference, no PVC, and the only difference is that the series was motion
corrected. The result is a clean null — Δ = +0.011, CI [−0.004, +0.022],
*r* = 0.11, *p* = 0.445, and ΔMSE +0.002 with the variance marginally lower.

Motion is half a voxel, correcting it removes 18.6 % of the image's gradient
energy, and the predicted input function does not change. The recommendation
follows without hedging: do not apply it here.

### 5.4 Motion correction partially undoes PVC

| Condition | Δ vs reference | *r* |
|---|---|---|
| `rl_shift_retrained` (PVC only) | +0.174 | 0.87 |
| `mc_rl_mcfirst_retrained` | +0.115 | 0.76 |
| `mc_rl_pvcfirst_retrained` | +0.108 | 0.75 |

Directly: `mcfirst` − `rl_shift` = −0.067, *p* = 2.6 × 10⁻¹⁰, *r* = 0.87.

Because registration on its own is neutral (§5.3), the only route by which it
can reduce the damage PVC does is by partially reversing it — interpolation
smoothing pushing the input back toward the distribution the model was fitted
on. This is not a benefit of motion correction; it is two errors partly
cancelling, and it should be reported as such.

**Ordering.** PVC first is better than motion correction first, by 0.0103,
*p* = 2.5 × 10⁻⁶, *r* = 0.65. Small, very consistent, and pointing the same
way: deconvolve before interpolation has smoothed the voxels.

### 5.5 The visible jump in napari is not motion

The apparent sideways jump around frames 25–34 was chased down and is an
artefact of viewing, not a movement of the animal. Two contributions:

1. Frame durations change at those boundaries, so the noise level changes
   abruptly between neighbouring frames, and the eye reads a step in texture as
   a step in position.
2. Tracer redistributes out of the blood pool over the same interval, so the
   intensity pattern genuinely changes shape without anything moving.

The direct test: the frame 27 − frame 26 difference image is salt-and-pepper
noise across the whole animal. A translation produces a crescent — bright on
one edge, dark on the opposite. There is no crescent. Nothing moved between
those frames beyond the sub-millimetre amount the transforms record.

### 5.6 Two measures that did not work

Recorded because they cost real time and should not be repeated.

- **Centre-of-mass tracking** returned a median displacement of 13.6 mm. That
  is tracer redistribution — the centre of mass of the whole animal genuinely
  moves as activity leaves the heart for the liver and bladder — not motion. It
  flagged all 70 scans for a reason that has nothing to do with the animal.
- **A purpose-built registration measure** returned p95 values up to 26 mm. It
  had been validated on a synthetic test that was too easy to be informative.

Both appear in `motion_qc.csv` as the `shift_*` and `*_displacement_*` columns
and should be ignored; the median `shift_reduction` across scans is 0.004,
i.e. the screen measures nothing. The magnitudes in §5.1 come from FALCON's own
fitted transforms, which is the right source: it is what the correction
actually applied.

### 5.7 Registration quality

22 frames across 20 scans were flagged as suspect (fitted rotation above 2°).
They cluster in frames 5–7, immediately after the start frame, where the bolus
has not fully arrived and there is little anatomy to register against. Raising
`motion.falcon.start_frame` from 5 to 7 would remove most of them, at the cost
of leaving frames 5–6 uncorrected. Not yet decided.

## 6. What is not trustworthy yet

**The effect is small, and the thesis should say so.** An RMSE reduction of
about 4 % at *r* ≈ 0.3 is a real result, not a large one, and §4.3 shows it
does not carry through to per-animal kinetic parameters. The claim the data
supports is that PVC plus retraining improves the predicted input function
modestly and mainly by making it more stable — not that it improves kinetic
modelling.

**Two summary statistics report different medians for the same condition.**
`summary.json` gives `baseline_retrained` as 0.516 and `comparisons.csv` as
0.580. Both are correct and they are different quantities: the first is the
median over all 700 scan × run rows, the second the median over 70 per-scan
means, and the distribution is right-skewed. **Quote 0.580**, the per-scan-mean
version, because that is what the statistics operate on — and use one of them
everywhere. A table showing 0.52 beside a test built on 0.58 will be asked
about.

**The iteration sensitivity is established for the deployed model only.**
Retraining ran at the primary iteration count alone; repeating the
cross-validation at *k* = 10 and 20 would have added 400 GPU jobs to answer the
weakest of the three sensitivity axes.

**An earlier `folds.json` bug invalidated all numbers produced before
10 September.** Each cluster job wrote its own fold partition, so stage 06 saw
one fold of 7 scans per condition, and different folds for different
conditions. Any figure carried over from before that date is wrong. Fixed in
`05_retrain.py` / `dlif/train.py` (the full partition is written) and guarded
in `06_evaluate.py` (on-disk fold directories are checked against the recorded
partition, and the default partition is used if they disagree).

**Smaller items.**

- Scan `AA1` has a corrected image but no row in `motion_qc.csv`, so §5 covers
  69 of 70. It was run separately during debugging and the resumed run skipped
  its QC row.
- `manifest.csv` has `motion_affected = False` for all 102 scans, while
  `configs/thesis.yaml` lists all 70 under `motion.affected_ids`. The config is
  what the subset resolution reads, so the behaviour is right, but the manifest
  column is stale and misleading.
- A stray `E:/` directory exists inside the repository, from a Windows path
  written as a relative path. It contains one log file and should be deleted.
- `pyproject.toml` still has `Repository = ".../<your-username>/pvc-dlif"`.
- Eleven source files have uncommitted changes.

## 7. Infrastructure and method work

Summarised because it is thesis-methods material, not just plumbing.

**`checkpoints_from`.** A condition can now declare that it trains nothing and
reuses another condition's weights. This is what makes the distribution-shift
measurement possible at inference cost instead of a full training grid, and it
is where the strongest result in §3 comes from. Stage 05 skips such conditions;
stage 06 resolves the checkpoint source at prediction time.

**`motion_order`.** A condition can now select which of motion correction and
PVC ran first (`mc_then_pvc`, default, or `pvc_then_mc`). Stage 07 had been
writing both orderings for all 70 scans, but the config schema had no way to
name the second one, so half that output was unreachable. The ordering
comparison the project description asks for is now expressible.

**PSF sensitivity.** The PSF was measured on a static point source at the
centre of the field and is treated as spatially invariant. Neither assumption
is exactly true, so the correction is re-run with the FWHM scaled to 0.9 and
1.1 and the result re-scored; the spread across scales is how much the
conclusion depends on the PSF being right. Measured FWHM is
0.864 / 0.874 / 0.994 mm against voxels of 0.5 / 0.5 / 0.597 mm.

The axial direction is the tight one: 0.994 / 0.597 = 1.67 voxels per FWHM,
between the 1.5-voxel undersampling threshold and the 2.0-voxel marginal
threshold. At scale 0.9 it falls to exactly 1.50 voxels, which is what raises
the warning during that run. The warning is expected and is itself part of the
result — it says the axial PSF is close to the sampling limit, so a deconvolution
that assumes a narrower kernel is operating at the edge of what the grid
supports.

Stage 08 now produces `psf_sweep.png` and `psf_sweep.csv`. The iteration sweep
was fixed at the same time: it had been plotting every corrected condition
against iteration count, which stacked the retrained, shift and motion
conditions on top of each other at x = 15 and drew them as one zigzag line. It
now separates series by method and model variant.

**FALCON on Windows.** Made to run end to end. The flags had been wrong (the
reference frame was passed to `-i`, `"2x1"` to `-sf`, and `-d` pointed at a
directory holding all 70 scans at once). Additional fixes: a hardlinked private
input directory per scan, `PYTHONUTF8=1` to survive the emoji in FALCON's own
log output on a cp1252 console, and a patch to falconz's `file_utilities.py:58`
where `icacls ... Everyone:(F)` fails on a non-English Windows — the SID
`*S-1-1-0:(F)` works on any locale. Scratch output is discarded after each run
with the transforms kept, which took disk use from about 41 GB to 6.7 GB.

**Springfield.** Stage 05 runs as a Kubernetes indexed job. Jobs were failing
with an NVML driver/library version mismatch on one node; pinning
`nodeSelector: kubernetes.io/hostname: barney` avoids it. This is a cluster
issue, not a pipeline one.

## 8. Next steps

In order of what unblocks what.

1. **Rewrite the introduction to match what was found.** The thesis currently
   argues a bias story: spill-out makes the blood pool read low, correct it and
   the predicted input function becomes more accurate. The data say the gain is
   mostly variance, that the predicted peak amplitude barely moves, and that
   the governing variable is whether model and input representation match. The
   result is good; the framing it was written for is not the one it supports.
2. **Do not retrain the `mc_*` conditions.** §5.3 settles the question at
   inference cost: registration on its own changes nothing measurable. Spending
   200 GPU jobs to establish it more precisely would be spending them on a null
   that is already clean.
3. **Ask C. Salomonsen** for the trained `DLIFNet_MAX` weights, and whether the
   reference input function is plasma or whole blood.
4. **Put the double-correction account to S. Kuttner** (§3.3). It is a claim
   about his own method, it carries two confirmed opposite predictions, and it
   is the part of this work most likely to change how the group thinks about
   DLIF.
5. **If one further experiment is run, it is a genuine open choice.** The loss
   weighting is cheap to test and is a real question about the published
   protocol, but §4.2 does not recommend it: the relative improvement is
   largest in the frames the loss already weights most. Any PVC-strength
   augmentation scheme must keep uncorrected images in the training mixture,
   since §3.1 shows training on corrected images alone and deploying on
   uncorrected ones is the worst cell in the whole design.
6. **Report back** that the `AdaptiveAvgPool3d` prediction was falsified. The
   expectation was that the 2026 architecture's global pooling would make it
   less sensitive to the input distribution shift than the 2024 model's fixed
   `Conv3d(128, 256, (4,3,3))`. It was not. The 2024 model degrades by 13 %
   (0.604 → 0.683) and the 2026 architecture by 26 % (0.533 → 0.671) — twice
   as much, in the opposite direction to the prediction. Global average pooling
   removes a dependence on *where* features occur, not on *how large* they are,
   and PVC changes magnitude. The two percentages are not a controlled
   comparison — the 2024 figure is one inference run of a model trained
   elsewhere, the 2026 figure ten runs of in-pipeline training — so the claim
   to make is the direction, not the ratio.
7. Housekeeping from §6: commit, delete the stray `E:/`, fix the manifest
   column and the repository URL, and decide on `start_frame`.

## 9. One-paragraph summary

Partial volume correction does what it is supposed to do to the images: about
60 % more blood-pool peak for about twice the noise, no negative voxels, with
Richardson–Lucy and Van-Cittert nearly indistinguishable. Carried through the
network it improves the predicted input function by about 4 % in RMSE
(*r* ≈ 0.3, *p* = 0.02–0.03), but not in the way the premise of this thesis
predicted: the predicted peak amplitude barely moves, and roughly 70 % of the
improvement is reduced variance rather than reduced bias — independently
trained models agree with each other far more, rather than the average
prediction becoming much better. What governs the behaviour is whether the
model and the input representation match. Crossing that match costs far more
than matching it gains, and it costs differently in each direction: shown
