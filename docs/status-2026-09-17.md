## 2. What partial volume correction does to the images

Measured per frame, then taken as a median over all frames and scans. Peak
recovery is the blood-pool peak after correction divided by before; noise
amplification is the same ratio for the within-region percentage standard
deviation.

| Input tag | Peak recovery | Noise amplification | Negative voxels after |
|---|---|---|---|
| `rl_i10` | 1.554 | 1.862 | 0.000 |
| `rl_i15` | 1.598 | 2.004 | 0.000 |
| `rl_i20` | 1.620 | 2.090 | 0.000 |
| `rvc_i10` | 1.541 | 1.997 | 0.000 |
| `rvc_i15` | 1.593 | 2.109 | 0.000 |
| `rvc_i20` | 1.627 | 2.173 | 0.000 |

The correction works, and it costs what deconvolution always costs: roughly
60 % more peak signal for roughly twice the noise. More iterations buy a little
more peak and a little more noise, monotonically, with no turning point inside
the range tested. Richardson–Lucy and Van-Cittert behave almost identically at
matched iteration counts; RL is marginally quieter. No negative voxels are
produced by either.

This is the input-side result and it stands on its own. Everything in §3 is
about what the network then does with these images.

## 3. Main result: RMSE of the predicted input function

Median RMSE per condition across 70 scans. Lower is better. The reference is
`baseline_retrained` at 0.533.

| Condition | Median RMSE | Δ vs reference | 95 % CI | Effect size *r* | *p* (Holm) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `baseline_pretrained` | 0.604 | −0.050 | [−0.108, 0.040] | −0.06 | 0.68 |
| **`baseline_retrained`** | **0.533** | — | — | — | — |
| `rl_retrained` | 0.520 | −0.016 | [−0.063, 0.004] | −0.26 | 0.26 |
| `rvc_retrained` | 0.534 | +0.003 | [−0.072, 0.036] | −0.22 | 0.26 |
| `rl_pretrained` | 0.683 | +0.020 | [−0.014, 0.094] | 0.30 | 0.22 |
| `rvc_pretrained` | 0.674 | +0.018 | [−0.022, 0.090] | 0.27 | 0.26 |
| `rl_i10_pretrained` | 0.676 | +0.020 | [−0.022, 0.124] | 0.29 | 0.22 |
| `rl_i20_pretrained` | 0.687 | +0.026 | [−0.012, 0.135] | 0.34 | 0.13 |
| `rvc_i10_pretrained` | 0.671 | +0.014 | [−0.029, 0.113] | 0.26 | 0.26 |
| `rvc_i20_pretrained` | 0.679 | +0.022 | [−0.020, 0.128] | 0.32 | 0.17 |
| `rl_shift_retrained` | 0.671 | **+0.188** | [0.129, 0.243] | **0.88** | **< 0.001** |
| `rvc_shift_retrained` | 0.663 | **+0.176** | [0.151, 0.247] | **0.89** | **< 0.001** |

Paired Wilcoxon signed-rank, Holm-corrected within metric; CI by percentile
bootstrap; effect size is rank-biserial.

Three things to read out of this table.

**Retraining on corrected images neither helps nor hurts, measurably.**
`rl_retrained` is 0.016 below the reference and `rvc_retrained` is 0.003 above
it. Neither is significant and both confidence intervals straddle zero. Caveat
in §6 — these two conditions have fewer completed training runs than the
reference, so this null is weaker than it looks.

**Applying an existing model to corrected images is clearly harmful.** The
`*_shift_*` conditions take the `baseline_retrained` weights — fitted on
uncorrected images — and feed them corrected ones. RMSE rises by 0.18–0.19,
*r* ≈ 0.88, *p* < 0.001. This is the largest and most reliable effect in the
dataset, and it is the one comparison that is exactly matched: same weights,
same runs, same folds, only the input differs.

**The pretrained conditions say the same thing less cleanly.** The published
2024 model (`DLIFNet.pt`) applied to corrected images sits at 0.67–0.69 against
0.60 on uncorrected. Same direction, smaller magnitude, but not significant
after correction and confounded by the model having been trained elsewhere on
different preprocessing. Note which model this is: the pretrained arm is the
2024 network throughout, because the trained `DLIFNet_MAX` weights have not
been obtained yet. The retrained arm is built from current upstream code and is
therefore the 2026 architecture.

So the finding is not "PVC does not help". It is that **the model and the input
representation have to match**, and the penalty for mismatching them is larger
than any benefit PVC has been shown to deliver.

## 4. Where the damage comes from

### 4.1 It is variance, not bias

Bias–variance decomposition of the squared error, averaged over scans.

| Condition | Repeats | MSE | Bias² | Variance | Variance share |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `baseline_retrained` | 10 | 0.576 | 0.359 | 0.217 | 37.7 % |
| `rl_shift_retrained` | 10 | 0.950 | 0.466 | 0.484 | 51.0 % |
| `rvc_shift_retrained` | 10 | 0.970 | 0.458 | 0.512 | 52.8 % |
| `rl_retrained` | 3 | 0.454 | 0.355 | 0.098 | 21.7 % |
| `rvc_retrained` | 3 | 0.455 | 0.362 | 0.094 | 20.5 % |

Taking `baseline_retrained` to `rl_shift_retrained` adds 0.374 of MSE. Of that,
0.107 is bias² and **0.267 — 71 % — is variance**. For RVC it is 75 %.

This is the interesting part. The obvious story would have been that corrected
images shift the model's predictions systematically, and that a recalibration
would fix it. That is not what happens. The predictions become *unstable*: the
same scan, predicted by models that differ only in training run, lands in
noticeably different places. Feeding a network an input distribution it was not
fitted on does not move it to a new consistent answer; it makes it stop
agreeing with itself.

A recalibration cannot repair that. This is an argument for retraining rather
than for a correction factor.

Read the last two rows with care — they have 3 repeats against the others' 10,
and variance measured over 3 runs systematically underestimates variance
measured over 10. The first three rows are like-for-like; comparisons against
the bottom two are not.

### 4.2 It lands on the peak

RMSE per time bin. The bolus peak sits in the first two bins.

| Time bin | `baseline_retrained` | `rl_retrained` | `rl_shift_retrained` | `baseline_pretrained` | `rl_pretrained` |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0–1 min | 0.943 | 0.868 | 1.148 | 0.832 | 0.939 |
| 1–2.5 min | 0.994 | 0.831 | **1.280** | 0.812 | 0.923 |
| 2.5–5.5 min | 0.348 | 0.319 | 0.492 | 0.423 | 0.466 |
| 5.5–40.5 min | 0.277 | 0.220 | 0.295 | 0.251 | 0.255 |
| ≥ 40.5 min | 0.337 | 0.248 | 0.343 | 0.237 | 0.235 |

The shift penalty is concentrated in 0–2.5 minutes and nearly gone by 5.5. PVC
changes the images most where the activity gradient is steepest — the peak —
so it is exactly there that the input distribution moves furthest from what the
model saw in training.

Worth noting against the training objective: the 2026 protocol's
`WeightedMSELoss` splits the curve at frames 0/25/34/42 with weights
0.4/0.7/1.0, each segment meaned separately. The per-frame effective weights
come out at 0.016 / 0.078 / 0.125 — the loss weights *late* frames about
eight times more heavily than the peak. The model is trained to care least
about the region where PVC does the most, and where all the error is.

### 4.3 The peak inversion

Median peak height of the prediction divided by the truth, and the predicted
peak's full width at half maximum (truth: 0.63–0.66 min).

| Condition | Peak height ratio | Predicted FWHM |
|---|---|---|
| `baseline_pretrained` | 0.934 | 1.005 |
| `rl_pretrained` | 0.890 | 1.610 |
| `rvc_pretrained` | 0.887 | 1.587 |
| `baseline_retrained` | 0.949 | 0.661 |
| `rl_retrained` | 0.963 | 0.681 |
| `rl_shift_retrained` | **1.054** | 0.653 |
| `rvc_shift_retrained` | **1.042** | 0.651 |

PVC raises the blood peak in the *image* by about 60 % (§2). What happens to
the *predicted* peak depends on which model sees it, and the two answers go in
opposite directions:

- The **pretrained** model responds to a taller, sharper blood pool by
  predicting a peak that is *lower* (0.93 → 0.89) and much *broader*
  (FWHM 1.01 → 1.61, against a truth of 0.63). It smears the bolus out.
- The **retrained weights on corrected input** (`*_shift_*`) overshoot
  instead — peak ratio above 1.0 — while keeping the width about right.

The pretrained behaviour is the counter-intuitive one and is worth a paragraph
in the thesis. A plausible mechanism: the network's temporal stage sees a
sharper, higher-contrast bolus than anything in its training distribution, and
the convolutional features saturate, so the temporal model receives a signal it
reads as a longer, flatter passage. It is a hypothesis, not a measurement — a
feature-activation check would be needed to support it.

The AUC results follow: the shift conditions overestimate total AUC by
4.1–4.4 % (*r* ≈ 0.97–0.98, *p* < 0.001), while the retrained conditions sit
within 2 % of truth and are not significant.

## 5. Motion

### 5.1 How much there is

FALCON, rigid registration, reference frame 34, registration started at frame 5.
69 scans, 2484 registered frames.

| Quantity | Median | p95 | Max |
|---|---|---|---|
| Per-frame translation | 0.271 mm | 0.852 mm | 4.714 mm |
| Per-frame rotation | 0.334° | 1.231° | 6.645° |
| Per-scan median translation | 0.280 mm | — | 0.491 mm |
| Per-scan max translation | 0.995 mm | — | 4.714 mm |

Voxels are 0.5 × 0.5 × 0.597 mm, so the median frame moves about half a voxel.
All 69 scans exceed 0.5 mm at some frame and 33 of 69 exceed 1 mm. The motion
is real, it is present everywhere, and it is small.

Only rigid registration is used, deliberately. Affine and deformable are not
volume-preserving, and the quantity of interest is a concentration — a
transform that changes a region's volume changes its SUV directly.

### 5.2 What correcting it costs

| Quantity | Before | After | Ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gradient energy | 0.1057 | 0.0863 | **0.814** |
| High-frequency share | 0.538 | 0.483 | 0.898 |

Motion correction removes **18.6 % of the image's gradient energy**. That is
interpolation smoothing, and it is the cost side of the ledger: the corrected
series is measurably blurrier than the original. Set against a median
displacement of half a voxel, it is not obvious that the trade is worth making.
This is the question the `mc_*` conditions exist to answer, and it is not yet
answered.

### 5.3 The visible jump in napari is not motion

The apparent sideways jump around frames 25–34 was chased down and is an
artefact of viewing, not a movement of the animal. Two contributions:

1. Frame durations change at those boundaries, so the noise level changes
   abruptly between neighbouring frames, and the eye reads a step in texture as
   a step in position.
2. Tracer redistributes out of the blood pool over the same interval, so the
   intensity pattern genuinely changes shape without anything moving.

The direct test: the frame 27 − frame 26 difference image is salt-and-pepper
noise across the whole animal. A translation produces a crescent — bright on
one edge, dark on the opposite. There is no crescent. Nothing moved between
those frames beyond the sub-millimetre amount the transforms record.

### 5.4 Two measures that did not work

Recorded because they cost real time and should not be repeated.

- **Centre-of-mass tracking** returned a median displacement of 13.6 mm. That
  is tracer redistribution — the centre of mass of the whole animal genuinely
  moves as activity leaves the heart for the liver and bladder — not motion. It
  flagged all 70 scans for a reason that has nothing to do with the animal.
- **A purpose-built registration measure** returned p95 values up to 26 mm. It
  had been validated on a synthetic test that was too easy to be informative.

Both appear in `motion_qc.csv` as the `shift_*` and `*_displacement_*` columns
and should be ignored; the median `shift_reduction` across scans is 0.004,
i.e. the screen measures nothing. The magnitudes in §5.1 come from FALCON's own
fitted transforms, which is the right source: it is what the correction
actually applied.

### 5.5 Registration quality

22 frames across 20 scans were flagged as suspect (fitted rotation above 2°).
They cluster in frames 5–7, immediately after the start frame, where the bolus
has not fully arrived and there is little anatomy to register against. Raising
`motion.falcon.start_frame` from 5 to 7 would remove most of them, at the cost
of leaving frames 5–6 uncorrected. Not yet decided.

## 6. What is not trustworthy yet

**The retrained grid is incomplete, and unevenly so.** The 2026 protocol calls
for 10 folds × 10 runs per condition. Actual prediction rows:

| Condition | Rows | Folds | Runs |
|---|---|---|---|
| `baseline_retrained` | 609 | 10 | 10 |
| `rl_retrained` | 210 | 10 | 3 |
| `rvc_retrained` | 210 | 10 | 3 |
| `rl_shift_retrained` | 609 | 10 | 10 |
| `rvc_shift_retrained` | 609 | 10 | 10 |

Complete would be 700 rows each. Two consequences:

1. The reference has roughly three times the repeats of the two conditions it is
   being compared against. The §3 null for `rl_retrained` / `rvc_retrained` and
   the §4.1 variance figures for those two rows are **not like-for-like** and
   should not be quoted until the grid is filled. About 153 runs remain.
2. The `*_shift_*` conditions are unaffected. They borrow the
   `baseline_retrained` checkpoints, so they are matched to the reference run
   for run. The headline finding rests on those, which is fortunate.

**An earlier `folds.json` bug invalidated all numbers produced before
10 September.** Each cluster job wrote its own fold partition, so stage 06 saw
one fold of 7 scans per condition, and different folds for different
conditions. Any figure carried over from before that date is wrong. Fixed in
`05_retrain.py` / `dlif/train.py` (the full partition is written) and guarded
in `06_evaluate.py` (on-disk fold directories are checked against the recorded
partition, and the default partition is used if they disagree).

**Smaller items.**

- Scan `AA1` has a corrected image but no row in `motion_qc.csv`, so §5 covers
  69 of 70. It was run separately during debugging and the resumed run skipped
  its QC row.
- `manifest.csv` has `motion_affected = False` for all 102 scans, while
  `configs/thesis.yaml` lists all 70 under `motion.affected_ids`. The config is
  what the subset resolution reads, so the behaviour is right, but the manifest
  column is stale and misleading.
- A stray `E:/` directory exists inside the repository, from a Windows path
  written as a relative path. It contains one log file and should be deleted.
- `pyproject.toml` still has `Repository = ".../<your-username>/pvc-dlif"`.
- Eleven source files have uncommitted changes.

## 7. Infrastructure and method work

Summarised because it is thesis-methods material, not just plumbing.

**`checkpoints_from`.** A condition can now declare that it trains nothing and
reuses another condition's weights. This is what makes the distribution-shift
measurement possible at inference cost instead of a full training grid, and it
is where the strongest result in §3 comes from. Stage 05 skips such conditions;
stage 06 resolves the checkpoint source at prediction time.

**`motion_order`.** A condition can now select which of motion correction and
PVC ran first (`mc_then_pvc`, default, or `pvc_then_mc`). Stage 07 had been
writing both orderings for all 70 scans, but the config schema had no way to
name the second one, so half that output was unreachable. The ordering
comparison the project description asks for is now expressible.

**PSF sensitivity.** The PSF was measured on a static point source at the
centre of the field and is treated as spatially invariant. Neither assumption
is exactly true, so the correction is re-run with the FWHM scaled to 0.9 and
1.1 and the result re-scored; the spread across scales is how much the
conclusion depends on the PSF being right. Measured FWHM is
0.864 / 0.874 / 0.994 mm against voxels of 0.5 / 0.5 / 0.597 mm.

The axial direction is the tight one: 0.994 / 0.597 = 1.67 voxels per FWHM,
between the 1.5-voxel undersampling threshold and the 2.0-voxel marginal
threshold. At scale 0.9 it falls to exactly 1.50 voxels, which is what raises
the warning during that run. The warning is expected and is itself part of the
result — it says the axial PSF is close to the sampling limit, so a deconvolution
that assumes a narrower kernel is operating at the edge of what the grid
supports.

Stage 08 now produces `psf_sweep.png` and `psf_sweep.csv`. The iteration sweep
was fixed at the same time: it had been plotting every corrected condition
against iteration count, which stacked the retrained, shift and motion
conditions on top of each other at x = 15 and drew them as one zigzag line. It
now separates series by method and model variant.

**FALCON on Windows.** Made to run end to end. The flags had been wrong (the
reference frame was passed to `-i`, `"2x1"` to `-sf`, and `-d` pointed at a
directory holding all 70 scans at once). Additional fixes: a hardlinked private
input directory per scan, `PYTHONUTF8=1` to survive the emoji in FALCON's own
log output on a cp1252 console, and a patch to falconz's `file_utilities.py:58`
where `icacls ... Everyone:(F)` fails on a non-English Windows — the SID
`*S-1-1-0:(F)` works on any locale. Scratch output is discarded after each run
with the transforms kept, which took disk use from about 41 GB to 6.7 GB.

**Springfield.** Stage 05 runs as a Kubernetes indexed job. Jobs were failing
with an NVML driver/library version mismatch on one node; pinning
`nodeSelector: kubernetes.io/hostname: barney` avoids it. This is a cluster
issue, not a pipeline one.

## 8. Next steps

In order of what unblocks what.

1. **Finish the retraining grid** — about 153 runs. Until this is done the
   retrained arm cannot be quoted (§6).
2. **Complete the PSF 1.1 arm**, then `03` → `04` → `06` → `08`. No retraining
   needed; all four PSF conditions use the published model.
3. **Score the motion conditions.** Stage 03 builds the `mc_*` input trees from
   data already on disk. `mc_rl_mcfirst_retrained` and
   `mc_rl_pvcfirst_retrained` then cost inference only and answer whether the
   ordering matters. Whether to spend a further 200 GPU jobs retraining
   `mc_baseline_retrained` and `mc_rl_retrained` is a decision worth taking
   after seeing those.
4. **Ask C. Salomonsen** for the trained `DLIFNet_MAX` weights, and whether the
   reference input function is plasma or whole blood.
5. **Report back** that the `AdaptiveAvgPool3d` prediction was falsified. The
   expectation was that the 2026 architecture's global pooling would make it
   less sensitive to the input distribution shift than the 2024 model's fixed
   `Conv3d(128, 256, (4,3,3))`. It was not. The 2024 model degrades by 13 %
   (0.604 → 0.683) and the 2026 architecture by 26 % (0.533 → 0.671) — twice
   as much, in the opposite direction to the prediction. Global average pooling
   removes a dependence on *where* features occur, not on *how large* they are,
   and PVC changes magnitude. The two percentages are not a controlled
   comparison — the 2024 figure is one inference run of a model trained
   elsewhere, the 2026 figure ten runs of in-pipeline training — so the claim
   to make is the direction, not the ratio.
6. Housekeeping from §6: commit, delete the stray `E:/`, fix the manifest
   column and the repository URL, and decide on `start_frame`.

## 9. One-paragraph summary

Partial volume correction does what it is supposed to do to the images —
about 60 % more blood-pool peak for about twice the noise, no negative voxels,
with Richardson–Lucy and Van-Cittert nearly indistinguishable. It does not
measurably improve the predicted input function when the network is retrained
on corrected images, and it clearly degrades the prediction when an existing
network is shown corrected images it was not fitted on: RMSE rises 0.18–0.19,
*r* ≈ 0.88, *p* < 0.001. That degradation is about 71 % variance rather than
bias, which means the network stops agreeing with itself rather than moving to
a new consistent answer, and so cannot be repaired by recalibration. The damage
is concentrated in the first 2.5 minutes, where the bolus peak is, and where
the published training loss happens to weight the error about eight times less
than it weights the late frames. Motion across the cohort is real but small — a
median of half a voxel — while correcting it removes 18.6 % of the image's
gradient energy, so whether it is worth applying is still an open question. The
retrained arm is not yet complete enough to quote.
